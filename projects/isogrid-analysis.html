Isogrid vs. Plain Cylinder: A First Look at FEA

↻ Drag to rotate · Scroll to zoom
Modeled in PTC Creo Parametric (isogridcylinder.glb) Orbit controls · Idle auto-rotate

Why I did this

I kept seeing isogrid structures on launch vehicles and wanted to understand why they’re used. I’m also learning FEA, so I modeled one and compared it with a plain cylinder (305.974 mm OD, 480.624 mm long, 10 mm wall in the baseline) to see where the isogrid actually helps.

What I ran

Each design was tested in axial tension, torsion (50 kN·m), and axial compression (500 kN). I started with an isogrid and a 10 mm tube. Then I compared at equal mass, because removing material and calling it a win isn’t a fair test.

Results

Before comparing designs, I checked that the plain-tube results matched hand calculations (PL/AE for tension, TL/GJ for torsion). They agreed within about 1%, so I trusted the setup.

Equal-mass comparison Plain tube Isogrid
Torsion, 12.4 kg: deflection 0.678 mm 0.781 mm
Torsion, 12.4 kg: peak stress 118 MPa 155 MPa
Torsion, 7.9 kg: deflection 1.09 mm 1.59 mm
Torsion, 7.9 kg: peak stress 192 MPa 339 MPa
Compression, about 8 kg: peak stress 160 MPa 398 MPa

The plain tube won in every case I ran. My first guess was that I had modeled something wrong. I hadn’t.

Why the isogrid lost

  • Stiffness comes from material at large radius. Tension, torsion, and bending are best handled by a continuous wall as far from the axis as possible, which is what a plain tube is.
  • The ribs are inefficient. A triangular lattice carries load at roughly a third of the efficiency of solid material per unit mass. In my 2 mm skin and 8 mm rib design, about half the mass was ribs.
  • Geometry raises local stress. Where skin and ribs meet, the load path is offset, which creates local bending and raises the peak stress, even with fillets.
  • Static analysis can’t see what isogrids are for. A linear static run only checks whether the material yields. It says nothing about buckling.

When an isogrid should win

Isogrids pay off for thin-walled, large-diameter shells (r/t above roughly 100) loaded in compression, bending, or external pressure. There, a plain wall would buckle at a small fraction of yield, and ribs raise bending stiffness far more than they add mass. My tube sits at r/t of about 36. Using NASA SP-8007’s knockdown factor (about 0.72 at this r/t), it would yield long before it buckles, so it isn’t in the regime where isogrids help.

Did the idealized model matter?

For the strength and stiffness cases, barely. A perfect circle with no defects shifts those results by a few percent at most. Buckling is the opposite. Real cylinders fail at roughly 20-70% of the perfect-shell prediction because they are imperfection-sensitive, and stiffened shells are less sensitive. My perfect model would hide exactly the advantage the isogrid has. Fatigue and manufacturing defects would also likely hurt the thin ribs more.

What I’d do next

  1. Run linear buckling on both designs at equal mass.
  2. Repeat with a much thinner wall at the same OD, where the isogrid should start to win.
  3. Run nonlinear buckling with an imperfection seeded from the first mode, to measure each design’s knockdown directly.
Takeaway: I went in expecting the isogrid to be better. The data showed that an isogrid is a solution to a stability problem, and my cylinder didn’t have one. Working out why was more useful than a result that confirmed my guess.
